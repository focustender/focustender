Hospital Readmissions & Utilization Risk

Readmission Risk & Outreach Value

Built on a public Kaggle dataset (25,000 diabetes hospital encounters), used here to demonstrate SQL analysis on a realistic healthcare business question, not proprietary or production data. Full write-up, schema, and queries on GitHub →

This project set out to answer a staffing and budget question: is post-discharge outreach worth targeting, and by what criteria. The business metric in view was readmission rate weighed against outreach cost, specifically whether a defensible high-risk segment exists that outreach could realistically move, rather than a blanket program applied to every discharge.

Recommendations

  1. Triage outreach by prior utilization history, not age, age alone would misroute effort toward older patients who aren't actually at elevated risk.
  2. Pilot a targeted outreach program against the high-risk segment, and validate the effectiveness assumption against a real pilot cohort rather than treating it as a budget number.
  3. Cross-reference readmission risk against income and access indicators before scaling outreach, a purely clinical program risks under-serving the population most likely to actually benefit.

Key Findings

Utilization history predicts risk far better than age does

Age barely moves readmission rate (44.2-49.6% across every band). Prior inpatient stays and ER visits do, climbing from 39.9% to 75.7% and 45.1% to 79.5%.

A defensible high-risk segment stands out clearly

16% of encounters (2+ prior inpatient or ER visits) run a 69.0% readmit rate versus 42.8% for everyone else. Illustrative cost-avoidance assumptions suggest 200-400 avoided readmissions worth $2.0M-$6.0M annually in gross avoided cost, not derived from this dataset, an estimate to validate, not a number to plan a budget around.

This dataset can't say whether risk is partly an access proxy

No socioeconomic fields exist here, so this measures clinical utilization risk only. Whether the high-risk segment is disproportionately access-limited rather than just clinically severe is a real open question worth real patient data.
